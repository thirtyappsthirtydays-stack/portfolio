01 · Discover

Why families keep putting it off

My initial idea was that families needed an app that stored all of their wealth information in one place to prepare for the great wealth transfer. Before designing anything, I talked to both generations: the people who built the money, and the people who would one day be left to sort it out.

What I learned

Insight 1

It matters, but nobody wants to go first

Both generations said the talk was important. Each was quietly waiting for the other to raise it.

Insight 2

It’s about readiness, not money

The worry underneath wasn’t a number. It was whether everyone would be okay, and whether a plan existed if something changed.

Insight 3

Small questions get answered. Big talks get postponed.

The “we need to sit down” moment rarely came. One easy question, asked over dinner, usually got an answer.

Insight 4 · The pivot

Visibility wasn’t the gap. Talking was.

My first concept tracked what a family owns. But a family that has never talked about money can’t fill in a tracker together.

02 · Define

Designing for Talya

I brought the research together into one primary persona: the person in the family who decides it’s time to talk. Her situation became a problem statement and four principles I tested every design against.

Problem statement

Talya needs a gentle way to start talking with her parents about money, care and their wishes, because waiting for the right moment means the talk happens in a crisis, or not at all.

How might we

Make the first question small enough to ask tonight, and the next one easy to follow?

Proto-personaTalya, 36Marketing manager · lives a flight away from her parents

“I don’t want it to sound like I’m asking about the will. I just want to know they’ll be okay.”

Family
Parents in their late sixties, one younger brother
Has tried
Bringing it up over the holidays, twice
Leans on
Family group chat, articles she saves and never reads

Goals

  • Know her parents’ wishes before a crisis forces the question
  • Start without making it about money or the will
  • Bring her brother into the same conversation

Frustrations

  • Every attempt turns into a joke or a change of subject
  • Doesn’t know which question to ask first
  • Worries she’ll sound like she’s after something

Proto-persona from research synthesis. Stock photo, not a real participant.

How I measured it
  • Task completion in moderated usability tests
  • Which concept participants preferred, and why
  • How confident families felt talking about money, before and after using the app

Four principles every design was tested against

01

Small first, deeper later

Every deck opens gently. Cards are tagged gentle, practical or deeper, and the five areas run in order.

02

No numbers needed

Questions ask about plans and confidence, never balances. Nobody has to share an amount to take part.

03

What’s said stays at the table

No account, no recordings, no answers saved. Progress lives in memory and resets when the app closes.

04

A tool, not a game

Calm language and no scores. A family talks a card through; nobody wins a round.

03 · Develop

Two concepts, tested, then distilled

I prototyped two shapes for the same job as clickable mobile flows and tested them before building anything. The preferred one became a simpler third: cards.

Concept A · 6 screens

Weekly questions

One question a week, sent to a parent or asked in person, with a place to log what they said.

Not taken forwardParticipants preferred the guided plan over weekly questions.
Concept B · 8 screens

A worry-led conversation plan

Start from what’s worrying you, get a step-by-step plan, practise first, then choose how to ask.

Preferred in testingIts order, from one small question to deeper ones, carried into the final design.
Final direction

Conversation cards

The plan’s sequence, distilled into decks a family reads aloud together, at the same table.

What I cut to shipThe AI practice partner, recordings and saved answers, for privacy and simplicity.

Design system

Every value is the one the app ships with

Two type families, a warm palette, a short spacing scale and a handful of components. Each component is a single piece of code, so a change lands on every screen at once.

A serif to talk, a sans to guide

Fraunces carries everything a family reads aloud to each other: questions, deck titles, area names. Figtree carries everything the app says to you: instructions, tips, tags and buttons.

Navy acts, gold marks progress, tints set the mood

A warm cream ground instead of white, so a hard topic feels like a kitchen table rather than a bank. Navy carries the text and the one action on each screen. Gold is kept for the logo and for progress.

Room to breathe, sized for thumbs

A hard conversation needs space around it. A fixed 20px margin, generous gaps and soft corners keep every screen calm, and the main controls are 44 to 54px tall.
